Bytes to tokens

A model never sees text. It sees a list of integers, and the tokenizer decides what those integers mean. Today you watch BPE build a vocabulary one merge at a time, then you write it yourself.

01 · THREE WAYS TO CUT TEXT

Words are too many, characters are too long

Any tokenizer has to choose the size of its pieces. Here is preaprobación under the three strategies. The last row is what GPT-4 actually does.

Word-level needs a token for every word form in every language, and gives up with [UNK] on anything new. Character-level never fails but makes every sequence 4–5× longer, so attention pays for spelling. Subword keeps common pieces whole and builds rare words from parts. Every modern LLM uses subwords; the question is which algorithm chooses the pieces.

02 · LAB A

Watch BPE learn

Byte Pair Encoding is a 1994 compression trick. Start with single characters. Count every adjacent pair. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token. Repeat. The ordered list of merges is the tokenizer.

Edit the corpus if you like, then press Merge and watch the winning pair fuse across the whole stream. Darker chips are newer tokens.

merges 0vocab 0tokens in stream 0compression 1.00
Pair counts, top 6. Next merge in orange.
Encode a new word with the merges learned so far

This lab merges Unicode characters so you can read the chips. Real GPT tokenizers merge raw bytes; section 03 explains why.

Two things to notice. Merges compound: hi + p only becomes possible after h + i exists, which is why encoding must replay merges in the order they were learned. And BPE is greedy: it never asks whether ipo is a meaningful unit, only whether it was frequent. That is where hip · otec · ario comes from.

03 · BYTES, NOT CHARACTERS

Why GPT starts from 256

Character-level BPE needs a base symbol for every character it might meet, and Unicode has 150,000. GPT-2's trick: operate on the UTF-8 bytes. The base vocabulary is exactly 256, any text in any script is representable, and there is no such thing as an unknown token. The cost is that non-ASCII characters start as several tokens.

One more thing real tokenizers do: the space attaches to the following word. In cl100k, Hello is id 9906 and  Hello (with the leading space) is id 22691, a different token. Two spaces before it become ' ' + ' Hello'. That is why prompts that end with a trailing space behave oddly: you have handed the model half a token.

05 · VOCABULARY SIZE IS A DIAL

Bigger vocabulary, shorter sequences, fatter embedding table

Every token id needs a row in the embedding matrix: vocab × d_model parameters, and the same again for the output layer unless the weights are tied. Move the model width and see what the three vocabularies cost, next to how many tokens they need for the Spanish sentence above.

Between GPT-2's vocabulary and GPT-4o's, at d_model = 4096, the embedding table alone differs by about 600M parameters. In exchange the Spanish sentence needs 25% fewer forward passes to generate. Vocabulary sizes keep growing because inference cost scales with sequence length, and parameters in an embedding table are cheap to store and cheap to compute.

06 · LAB B

BPE counts. WordPiece asks "is this surprising?"

BERT's WordPiece uses the same merge loop with a different score. BPE merges the pair with the highest count(A,B). WordPiece merges the pair with the highest count(A,B) / (count(A) · count(B)): how much more often A and B appear together than you would expect if they were independent.   1. get_pairs(tokens): count adjacent pairs. collections.Counter does most of the work.
  2. merge_pair(tokens, pair, new_id): one left-to-right pass, replacing non-overlapping matches.
  3. BPETokenizer.__init__: the 256-byte base vocabulary.
  4. train(text, num_merges): the loop from Lab A, on text.encode("utf-8"). Stop early when no pair repeats.
  5. encode / decode: replay merges in learned order; concatenate bytes and decode with errors="replace".
  6. wordpiece_score and fertility: the two formulas from sections 04 and 06.

Suggested order: primitives first (tests 1–4), then the class, then the metrics. 09 · BACK TO YOUR WORK

Where this touches a KB and RAG system

  • Chunking. If you chunk documents by characters or words, chunks in Spanish carry fewer tokens of budget than you think. Chunk by tokens, and use the tokenizer of the model that will read the chunk.
  • Two tokenizers per pipeline. Your embedding model and your generator almost certainly have different tokenizers. A chunk that fits one may not fit the other.
  • Identifiers and codes. Account numbers, IBANs and product codes fragment into many tokens with no semantic value. Expect retrieval on them to behave differently from retrieval on prose.
  • Cost. Your per-document cost is 1.3–1.6× what an English benchmark suggests. Measure it with tiktoken on your own corpus rather than trusting published averages.

When your tests pass, write a 3–5 line note: one thing you believed about tokens that turned out to be wrong, and one decision in your real pipeline you would now make differently.
